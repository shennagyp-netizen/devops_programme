DAY 3 — KUBERNETES

EPISODE D3.1 — Kubernetes Mental Model

Speaker A: Kubernetes becomes much easier when you stop seeing YAML and start seeing a reconciliation system.

Speaker B: Desired state versus actual state.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So the picture is:

Speaker A: Desired State -> Controller -> Observe -> Act -> Converge.

Speaker B: That is the mental model.

Speaker A: Yes. YAML is a way to describe desired state. The control loops are the part that keeps the system working toward it.

Speaker B: Stage one: desired state.

Speaker A: A Deployment can say it wants three replicas.

Speaker B: That is a condition the system should keep trying to satisfy.

Speaker A: Exactly. It is not a command that runs once and disappears.

Speaker B: Stage two: controller.

Speaker A: Controllers watch resources and reconcile differences between what should exist and what actually exists.

Speaker B: So a Deployment controller is responsible for keeping the workload aligned with the requested state.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Stage three: observe.

Speaker A: Kubernetes has to know what is happening now.

Speaker B: Pods exist or do not exist.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Pods may be Pending, Running or failing.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Scheduling, image pulls and readiness all affect actual state.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Stage four: act.

Speaker A: The controller takes an action that should reduce the difference.

Speaker B: Create a replacement pod.

Speaker A: Or update a resource.

Speaker B: Or remove an object that should no longer exist.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Stage five: converge.

Speaker A: The loop repeats. Observe. Compare. Act. Observe again.

Speaker B: So reconciliation is continuous.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Prediction.

Speaker A: I delete one pod from a Deployment that wants three replicas. Is that deletion permanent?

Speaker B: No. The Deployment controller should observe that only two replicas remain and create a replacement.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: But the new pod might still fail.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: Maybe the image cannot be pulled.

Speaker A: Maybe the process crashes.

Speaker B: Maybe readiness never succeeds.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So reconciliation means Kubernetes keeps trying. It does not guarantee success.

Speaker A: That distinction is critical.

Speaker B: A controller can do the right action over and over while the desired state stays impossible.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Real-life analogy?

Speaker A: Imagine hotel management wants ten rooms ready.

Speaker B: Two rooms become unavailable.

Speaker A: Management observes the difference and acts: clean, repair or reassign rooms.

Speaker B: If the building has no water, management can keep trying but still cannot satisfy the target.

Speaker A: Good. The desired state may be impossible under the current conditions.

Speaker B: That sounds like Kubernetes.

Speaker A: Because it is.

Speaker B: Prediction pause.

Speaker A: I set replicas to five, but the cluster has no available capacity. Should five healthy pods necessarily appear?

Speaker B: No. The controller can keep trying, but scheduling constraints can make the desired state impossible at that moment.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So 'I asked for five' is not evidence that five are actually running.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: I need to inspect actual state.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Another prediction.

Speaker A: The Deployment says three replicas. The cluster shows three pods. Are we done?

Speaker B: Not necessarily. They might be Running but not Ready, or one may be restarting, or the application may still be failing.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So desired count is only one piece of the truth.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: What makes Kubernetes different from a script that creates three containers?

Speaker A: The system keeps observing and reconciling after the initial creation. The desired state remains an ongoing target.

Speaker B: So a script says, 'do this now.'

Speaker A: And reconciliation says, 'keep reality aligned with this condition.'

Speaker B: That is the important shift.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Controlled failure?

Speaker A: Create a small Deployment.

Speaker B: Verify the desired replica count and actual running state.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Delete one pod deliberately.

Speaker A: Predict what the controller should do.

Speaker B: Create a replacement.

Speaker A: Observe it.

Speaker B: Verify that it becomes Ready.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then create one impossible condition, such as a bad image reference.

Speaker A: Predict the difference.

Speaker B: The controller should keep the desired replica count, but the replacement can fail to start because the image cannot be pulled.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So the controller is still working.

Speaker A: Yes. The desired state is just not feasible.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Create the Deployment.

Speaker B: Watch the pods.

Speaker A: Scale it.

Speaker B: Good.

Speaker A: Delete one pod.

Speaker B: Predict the replacement.

Speaker A: Observe.

Speaker B: Then make the image reference invalid in a disposable test workload.

Speaker A: Predict the result.

Speaker B: Observe the failed replacement and the repeated reconciliation attempts.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Restore the valid image.

Speaker A: Verify that the workload returns to the expected healthy state.

Speaker B: So the lab teaches both success and impossible desired state.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Recall.

Speaker A: What does YAML describe?

Speaker B: Desired state.

Speaker A: What does the controller do?

Speaker B: It observes actual state, compares it with desired state and takes actions to reduce the difference.

Speaker A: Is reconciliation one action?

Speaker B: No. It is an ongoing loop.

Speaker A: Can reconciliation fail to reach the desired state?

Speaker B: Yes. The controller can keep trying while the desired state is blocked by an image, resource, configuration or scheduling problem.

Speaker A: Final challenge.

Speaker B: I delete a Deployment pod and another appears, but the new pod never becomes Ready.

Speaker A: Your model?

Speaker B: Desired State -> Controller -> Observe -> Act -> Converge. Kubernetes is doing the replacement work, so I now inspect why the replacement cannot become healthy instead of blaming reconciliation itself.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So Kubernetes is not mainly YAML.

Speaker A: It is a reconciliation system that uses declarations to drive control loops.

Speaker B: Run the lab, delete one pod, watch the replacement, then make one disposable desired-state condition impossible and explain the evidence.

EPISODE D3.2 — Kubernetes Networking

Speaker A: We have a Kubernetes controller keeping workloads alive.

Speaker B: Now the next question is: how does traffic find the right workload when Pods can disappear and return with new IP addresses?

Speaker A: Exactly. The visual for today is:

Speaker B: Service -> Selector -> Endpoint set -> Pod -> Evidence.

Speaker A: Five boundaries. Each answers a different question.

Speaker B: Start with the Service.

Speaker A: A Service gives callers a stable identity while the backend Pods can change.

Speaker B: So the client should not need the current Pod IP.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: That is the main abstraction.

Speaker A: Make a prediction.

Speaker A: One Pod is deleted. Should a client that uses the Service normally need to learn the new Pod IP?

Speaker B: No. The Service abstraction should keep the caller on the stable path while Kubernetes updates the available endpoint set.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Stage two: selector.

Speaker A: The Service needs a rule that says which Pods belong to its backend set.

Speaker B: Labels and selectors.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So a Service can exist while matching zero Pods.

Speaker A: Yes. That is an important failure state.

Speaker B: The Service name can resolve correctly and still have no usable backends.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Prediction.

Speaker A: The Service is healthy, DNS resolves, but there are no endpoints.

Speaker B: I inspect the selector and the labels on the Pods before blaming the network path.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: If the selector is wrong, the abstraction is correct but the backend set is empty.

Speaker A: Precisely.

Speaker B: Stage three: endpoint set.

Speaker A: Kubernetes maintains information about which Pod endpoints currently back the Service path.

Speaker B: So this is where the changing Pod set becomes concrete.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: If a Pod is not ready, it may be excluded from normal ready traffic.

Speaker A: Right. The exact behavior depends on the controller and Service implementation, but the operational model is that traffic-ready endpoints are not simply every Pod object that happens to exist.

Speaker B: So running is not the same as ready for Service traffic.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: That sounds like the liveness versus readiness lesson.

Speaker A: It is the networking consequence of that distinction.

Speaker B: A Pod can exist, the process can be running, and the Service can still have a smaller endpoint set.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Stage four: Pod.

Speaker A: Eventually the request must reach a concrete Pod endpoint.

Speaker B: The Pod owns the process and its network namespace.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: But the caller should not have to manage the Pod lifecycle.

Speaker A: Correct. That is exactly what the Service abstraction is trying to hide.

Speaker B: Real-life analogy?

Speaker A: Think of a hospital department.

Speaker B: A visitor asks for cardiology.

Speaker A: The visitor does not need today's room number for the doctor.

Speaker B: Reception routes them to the current available room.

Speaker A: Exactly. The department identity is stable; the assignment changes.

Speaker B: Now the evidence stage.

Speaker A: When traffic fails, do not jump straight to the Pod.

Speaker B: First Service lookup.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then selector match.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then endpoint membership.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then real traffic to the backend.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: That is the diagnostic sequence.

Speaker A: It keeps the abstractions separate.

Speaker B: Failure one: the Service name does not resolve.

Speaker A: Start with DNS and Service identity.

Speaker B: Failure two: Service resolves but has no endpoints.

Speaker A: Check selector and Pod labels.

Speaker B: Failure three: endpoint set contains no ready Pod.

Speaker A: Inspect Pod readiness, startup and the conditions that remove it from the ready path.

Speaker B: Failure four: endpoints exist but requests still fail.

Speaker A: Now inspect the Pod process, container port, network policy, application listener and the actual request path.

Speaker B: So the endpoint list narrows the problem, but it does not prove the application is healthy.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Prediction pause.

Speaker A: A Service has two endpoints. We delete one Pod.

Speaker B: The endpoint set should shrink and then recover when the replacement Pod becomes ready.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Another one.

Speaker A: We change the Service selector so it matches no Pods. What do we predict?

Speaker B: The Service identity may still resolve, but the backend endpoint set should become empty.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Another.

Speaker A: The Pod is running but readiness fails.

Speaker B: The Pod can exist while being removed from the ready traffic path.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: That is the visible connection between health and networking.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Now a subtle boundary.

Speaker A: A Service and an Ingress are not the same abstraction.

Speaker B: Service gives a stable in-cluster service path.

Speaker A: And Ingress or another gateway layer can expose and route external HTTP traffic according to another set of rules.

Speaker B: So if external traffic fails, I should not immediately blame the Service.

Speaker A: Correct. Start at the layer where the evidence says the request stopped.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Deploy two Pods with a label set.

Speaker B: Create a Service with a selector matching those labels.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Verify the Service name resolves.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Inspect the endpoint set.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Send a real request through the Service.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Delete one Pod.

Speaker A: Observe the endpoint set change.

Speaker B: Wait for the replacement to become ready.

Speaker A: Verify the Service still works.

Speaker B: Then deliberately change the selector so it matches nothing.

Speaker A: Predict the result before observing it.

Speaker B: Observe the empty endpoint set.

Speaker A: Restore the selector and prove the request path recovers.

Speaker B: That is much stronger than memorizing Service YAML.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: What should the lab record contain?

Speaker A: Service name. Selector. Pod labels. Endpoint set before and after. Pod readiness. Real request result. Recovery evidence.

Speaker B: So the whole network model can be written as one path.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Service -> Selector -> Endpoint set -> Pod -> Evidence.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Recall.

Speaker A: What problem does a Service solve?

Speaker B: It gives a stable network identity over a changing set of backend Pods.

Speaker A: What does the selector do?

Speaker B: It determines which Pods are candidates for the Service backend set.

Speaker A: What does the endpoint set tell you?

Speaker B: Which backend endpoints Kubernetes currently considers available for the Service path.

Speaker A: Why can a running Pod still fail to receive Service traffic?

Speaker B: It may not be ready, or it may not match the Service selector, or another network condition may exclude it.

Speaker A: Final challenge.

Speaker B: The Service name resolves, but users get no response.

Speaker A: Your model?

Speaker B: Service -> Selector -> Endpoint set -> Pod -> Evidence. I check the Service identity, selector match, endpoint membership and Pod readiness before moving into the application process or lower network layers.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: And after deleting a Pod?

Speaker A: What proves recovery?

Speaker B: The replacement becomes ready, the endpoint set returns to the expected state, and the real request succeeds again.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Kubernetes networking is not a Service object.

Speaker A: It is the relationship among stable identity, selection, endpoint state and Pod lifecycle.

Speaker B: Run the lab, break one boundary, and prove the Service path converges again.

EPISODE D3.3 — Kubernetes Configuration and Storage

Speaker A: Configuration and persistent data should not be confused with the container image.

Speaker B: Because configuration changes more often than the image?

Speaker A: Often, yes. ConfigMaps hold non-secret configuration. Secrets provide a mechanism for sensitive values, but security still depends on how those values are handled. Volumes provide storage behavior beyond the temporary container filesystem.

Speaker A: Think of a theater production. The actors are the containers. The script configuration is separate. The stage props and persistent archive are another concern entirely.

Speaker A: Real-life example: replacing an actor should not erase the production's historical records.

Speaker A: Lab: mount configuration and persistent storage separately and observe what survives a container restart.
Speaker B: Configuration and storage feel like two different kinds of “outside the container.”

Speaker A: That's a good instinct. Configuration describes how the application should behave. Persistent storage is data the application needs to survive process replacement.

Speaker B: ConfigMap for normal configuration. Secret for sensitive values.

Speaker A: Yes, with the caveat that putting something in a Secret object doesn't automatically make your whole secret-handling process secure. Access control, transport, logging, and the way applications consume the value still matter.

Speaker B: Right. Security isn't a magic type.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Theater analogy?

Speaker A: The actors can change without rewriting the script. Props and archived records are another thing entirely. You don't throw away the production's history because one actor left.

Speaker B: Prediction. I restart a pod and a generated file disappears.

Speaker A: Where was that file stored?

Speaker B: If it lived only in the container's writable filesystem, I shouldn't assume it survives.

Speaker A: Good. Now suppose the file is on a persistent volume.

Speaker B: Then replacing the pod may leave the data available to the replacement, assuming the volume attachment and application behavior are correct.

Speaker A: Exactly. “Persistent” doesn't mean “immune to bugs.”

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Mount configuration separately from persistent data. Change the configuration, restart the workload, and verify what changed. Then write data, replace the pod, and verify what survived.

Speaker B: The lesson is to know which state belongs to the process and which state belongs to the workload.

Speaker A: That's the lesson. Containers and pods are allowed to be disposable. Your data may not be.


EPISODE D3.4 — Kubernetes Health and Scaling

Speaker A: A running process is not necessarily ready to serve traffic.

Speaker B: The classic 'technically alive, practically useless' state.

Speaker A: Exactly. Startup, readiness, and liveness probes answer different questions.

Speaker A: Imagine a restaurant. A cook may have arrived for work, but the kitchen is not ready because the ovens are still cold. A readiness check should keep new orders away until the kitchen can actually serve them.

Speaker A: Resource requests and limits help the scheduler and runtime make capacity decisions. Horizontal scaling adds replicas when the workload requires it.

Speaker A: Lab: create probes, impose a startup delay, scale replicas, and perform a rolling update.
Speaker B: This is the “alive but useless” problem.

Speaker A: Exactly. A process can be running and still be unable to serve traffic.

Speaker B: So now we need readiness and liveness.

Speaker A: And startup behavior. Those checks answer different questions. A workload might need time to initialize. It might be alive but not ready. Or it might be wedged and need a restart.

Speaker B: Restaurant analogy again.

Speaker A: A cook can be standing in the kitchen and still be unable to serve because the ovens aren't ready. “Person present” is not the same as “kitchen ready.”

Speaker B: Prediction pause. If readiness fails but liveness succeeds, should traffic still be sent there?

Speaker A: No, not if the readiness mechanism is wired correctly. The workload can stay running while being removed from the set of traffic-ready endpoints.

Speaker B: That's elegant.

Speaker A: It's also extremely practical. It lets you use health information to control traffic without necessarily killing the process.

Speaker B: And resources?

Speaker A: Requests and limits influence scheduling and resource protection. If you ignore resource behavior, scaling becomes guesswork.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Create a workload with a deliberate startup delay. Add a readiness probe. Watch when it enters the ready set. Then scale replicas and observe how capacity changes. Finally perform a rolling update and watch the old and new versions overlap.

Speaker B: So the system has state transitions I can actually observe.

Speaker A: Yes. That's why you should watch the objects instead of only reading configuration files.


EPISODE D3.5 — Kubernetes Failure Lab

Speaker A: Today we deliberately produce the ugly statuses engineers learn to fear: CrashLoopBackOff, ImagePullBackOff, OOMKilled, bad selectors, and failed readiness.

Speaker B: This feels like learning medicine by reading symptoms and then creating them in a simulator.

Speaker A: That is actually a very good analogy.

Speaker A: Real-life incident: if a hospital monitor says 'patient unstable,' you do not restart random machines. You collect vital signs and locate the failing subsystem.

Speaker A: Same here: inspect events, describe the object, logs, probes, resources, image state, and service endpoints.

Speaker A: Lab: break one thing at a time. Before running diagnostics, write down your hypothesis and predicted evidence.

Speaker B: The point is to train the diagnostic sequence, not memorize the names of red statuses.

Speaker A: Exactly.
Speaker B: I recognize the statuses now, but I don't want to memorize a red-flag dictionary.

Speaker A: Good. A status name is a clue, not a diagnosis.

Speaker B: CrashLoopBackOff means something about repeated restarts.

Speaker A: Yes. But then you ask why. Logs? Exit code? Configuration? Dependency? Resource limit?

Speaker B: ImagePullBackOff points somewhere else.

Speaker A: Image retrieval, registry access, image name, credentials, network path. Again, use the status to narrow the search.

Speaker B: OOMKilled means memory pressure or a memory limit event.

Speaker A: Right. Then inspect requests, limits, actual usage, and whether the workload's memory behavior changed.

Speaker B: Hospital monitor analogy?

Speaker A: “Patient unstable” is not a diagnosis. It's an alarm that tells you to inspect vital signs and identify the failing subsystem.

Speaker B: Prediction. Bad Service selector.

Speaker A: What would you expect?

Speaker B: The Service may exist, DNS may resolve, but there may be no endpoints behind it.

Speaker A: Good. That is a much more useful hypothesis than “Kubernetes networking is broken.”

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Break exactly one thing at a time. Bad image name. Then bad selector. Then failing readiness. Then a memory limit. Before every change, write the predicted evidence.

Speaker B: And then compare prediction to reality.

Speaker A: Exactly. The mismatch is often the most valuable part of the exercise.

Speaker B: Because it exposes where my mental model was wrong.

Speaker A: Now you're using Kubernetes as a laboratory instead of as a YAML typing competition.
