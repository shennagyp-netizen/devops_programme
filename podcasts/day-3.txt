DAY 3 — KUBERNETES

EPISODE D3.1 — Kubernetes Mental Model

Speaker A: Kubernetes becomes much easier when you stop seeing YAML and start seeing a reconciliation system.

Speaker B: Desired state versus actual state.

Speaker A: Exactly. You declare what you want; controllers keep trying to make reality converge toward it.

Speaker A: Think of hotel management. The desired state is 'ten rooms ready.' If one room becomes unavailable, management works to restore the target. Kubernetes performs this kind of reconciliation continuously.

Speaker B: So the magic is actually control loops.

Speaker A: Yes. Pods, deployments, ReplicaSets, scheduling and controllers become understandable once the loop is clear.

Speaker A: Real-life example: if an employee leaves unexpectedly, a good operation does not merely record that fact; it activates a process to restore service capacity.

Speaker A: Lab: create a deployment, scale it, delete a pod, and watch Kubernetes recreate it.



EPISODE D3.2 — Kubernetes Networking

Speaker A: Now we combine the networking foundation with Kubernetes.

Speaker B: I knew the networking day would come back to collect its debt.

Speaker A: Happily, it pays interest.

Speaker A: A request may travel through DNS, an external load balancer, ingress, a Service, and a selected Pod. Each abstraction solves a different problem.

Speaker A: Real-life analogy: a hospital visitor knows a department name, not the exact bed occupied by the doctor today. Reception, department routing, and internal assignment happen in layers.

Speaker A: The Service gives stable discovery while pods can come and go.

Speaker A: Lab: deploy two pods, expose them through a Service, inspect endpoints, then kill one pod and observe the routing behavior.



EPISODE D3.3 — Kubernetes Configuration and Storage

Speaker A: Configuration and persistent data should not be confused with the container image.

Speaker B: Because configuration changes more often than the image?

Speaker A: Often, yes. ConfigMaps hold non-secret configuration. Secrets provide a mechanism for sensitive values, but security still depends on how those values are handled. Volumes provide storage semantics beyond the ephemeral container filesystem.

Speaker A: Think of a theater production. The actors are the containers. The script configuration is separate. The stage props and persistent archive are another concern entirely.

Speaker A: Real-life example: replacing an actor should not erase the production's historical records.

Speaker A: Lab: mount configuration and persistent storage separately and observe what survives a container restart.



EPISODE D3.4 — Kubernetes Health and Scaling

Speaker A: A running process is not necessarily ready to serve traffic.

Speaker B: The classic 'technically alive, practically useless' state.

Speaker A: Exactly. Startup, readiness, and liveness probes answer different questions.

Speaker A: Imagine a restaurant. A cook may have arrived for work, but the kitchen is not ready because the ovens are still cold. A readiness check should keep new orders away until the kitchen can actually serve them.

Speaker A: Resource requests and limits help the scheduler and runtime make capacity decisions. Horizontal scaling adds replicas when the workload requires it.

Speaker A: Lab: create probes, impose a startup delay, scale replicas, and perform a rolling update.



EPISODE D3.5 — Kubernetes Failure Lab

Speaker A: Today we deliberately produce the ugly statuses engineers learn to fear: CrashLoopBackOff, ImagePullBackOff, OOMKilled, bad selectors, and failed readiness.

Speaker B: This feels like learning medicine by reading symptoms and then creating them in a simulator.

Speaker A: That is actually a very good analogy.

Speaker A: Real-life incident: if a hospital monitor says 'patient unstable,' you do not restart random machines. You collect vital signs and locate the failing subsystem.

Speaker A: Same here: inspect events, describe the object, logs, probes, resources, image state, and service endpoints.

Speaker A: Lab: break one thing at a time. Before running diagnostics, write down your hypothesis and predicted evidence.

Speaker B: The point is to train the diagnostic sequence, not memorize the names of red statuses.

Speaker A: Exactly.
