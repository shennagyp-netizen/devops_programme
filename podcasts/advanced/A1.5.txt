EPISODE A1.5 — Global Traffic Without Magic

Speaker A: We have users in Cairo, London and Singapore.

Speaker B: Three regions.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: So we send each user to the nearest region.

Speaker A: Maybe.

Speaker B: Again.

Speaker A: Distance is one factor.

Speaker B: What else?

Speaker A: Capacity. Health. Data location. Current failures. Sometimes cost.

Speaker B: So "nearest region" is not enough.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: Let's make the simple version first.

Speaker A: A user makes a request.

Speaker B: A traffic layer chooses a region.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: That region serves the request.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: If the region is unhealthy, traffic may move elsewhere.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Sounds simple.

Speaker A: Until the backup region is full.

Speaker B: Right.

Speaker A: Or the user needs data that is far away.

Speaker B: Or the health check says the region is healthy but one business function is broken.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So traffic routing needs good signals.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Human example?

Speaker A: A company has three shops.

Speaker B: One in each city.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: A customer wants to buy something.

Speaker A: You send them to the closest shop.

Speaker B: Unless that shop has no stock.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Or is closed.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Or has a huge queue.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So "closest" is only useful if the shop can actually serve the customer.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: And if the backup shop is already full, sending more customers there can break it too.

Speaker A: That is the failover trap.

Speaker B: Good.

Speaker A: Now the technical version.

Speaker B: We might use DNS, a global load balancer, anycast, application routing, or a mix.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: But the technology is less important than the decision model.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: What is the route decision based on?

Speaker A: Health, latency, capacity, and policy.

Speaker B: Policy could be geography or business rules.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: What is health?

Speaker A: Does the service meet the checks that matter for traffic?

Speaker B: Not just "process is running."

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: Maybe the region can serve the main API but not the payment path.

Speaker A: Then the check must match the traffic decision.

Speaker B: Good.

Speaker A: This is why health checks need care.

Speaker B: Same lesson again.

Speaker A: It keeps coming back because the system keeps depending on it.

Speaker B: Let's make a failure.

Speaker A: Region one is healthy.

Speaker B: Region two is healthy.

Speaker A: Region three has high latency.

Speaker B: We send new users away from region three.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then region one fails.

Speaker A: Now what?

Speaker B: Send traffic to region two.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: But region two was at 85% capacity before the failover.

Speaker A: So?

Speaker B: We need to know if it can take the extra traffic.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: If not, the failover makes the outage larger.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: So global traffic and capacity planning are one problem.

Speaker A: Very much.

Speaker B: What about DNS TTL?

Speaker A: It affects how fast some clients can learn a new answer.

Speaker B: So a traffic change may not reach everyone at once.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: Which means failover can be gradual.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: That may be good for testing.

Speaker A: It can be.

Speaker B: But it can also make an incident harder to control.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Let's talk about partial failover.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Move ten percent of traffic to the backup.

Speaker A: Then watch.

Speaker B: If it looks good, move more.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So we can use traffic shifting as a safe rollout tool.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Canary.

Speaker A: One example.

Speaker B: Regional canary.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: The same system can help both releases and failures.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Prediction.

Speaker A: Go.

Speaker B: Region one fails. Region two has enough raw CPU, but its database is already near its limit. Can we safely send all traffic there?

Speaker A: No.

Speaker B: Because the database becomes the limit.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: So I need to test the full request path, not just host CPU.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Another prediction. We change routing, but some users still go to the old region for a while.

Speaker A: Why might that happen?

Speaker B: Caches. DNS TTL. Client behavior. Other routing layers.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So "I changed the route" does not mean every request changed instantly.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: What about user session state?

Speaker A: Important.

Speaker B: If the user logs in to region one and then gets sent to region two, region two needs access to the session state or a design that avoids this problem.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: So global traffic creates application state questions.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Another hidden dependency.

Speaker A: Distributed systems are full of them.

Speaker B: What about data?

Speaker A: If the user's data lives mainly in region one and the request moves to region two, the application must still get the data.

Speaker B: Which can add latency.

Speaker A: Or create a consistency issue.

Speaker B: So traffic routing cannot be designed alone.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: Network, data, state, capacity, health.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Human example?

Speaker A: A shop chain.

Speaker B: Okay.

Speaker A: The closest shop closes.

Speaker B: Customer goes to the next shop.

Speaker A: But if that shop has no stock, the customer is still unhappy.

Speaker B: So moving the customer is not the same as solving the request.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: That's the whole point.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Mac exercise?

Speaker A: Draw two regions.

Speaker B: Give each one capacity.

Speaker A: Give each one a database path.

Speaker B: Give each one a health check.

Speaker A: Then simulate region one failing.

Speaker B: Decide how traffic moves.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then simulate region two at 80% before failover.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Ask whether the plan still works.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Then simulate stale DNS.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: See how much traffic may still reach region one.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: What should success look like?

Speaker A: You can explain why traffic goes where it goes, what signal moves it, how much traffic can move safely, and what user state and data must survive the move.

Speaker B: Good.

Speaker A: Recall.

Speaker B: Let's go.

Speaker A: Why is closest region not enough?

Speaker B: Because health, capacity, data location, latency and policy also matter.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: What makes failover risky?

Speaker B: The backup region may not have enough capacity or the needed data and dependencies.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Why can users keep reaching an old region?

Speaker B: Cached DNS or other routing layers may still use the old decision for a while.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Why does session state matter?

Speaker B: A user moved to another region still needs access to the state required for the request.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: What is the main idea?

Speaker B: Traffic routing is part of the whole system design. It cannot be separated from capacity, data, state and health.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Final challenge?

Speaker A: Design a two-region failover plan.

Speaker B: Then try to break it.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Make one region fail.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Make the other nearly full.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Then see if the design still protects users.

Speaker A: That's the work.
