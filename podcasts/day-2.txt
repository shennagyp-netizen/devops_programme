DAY 2 — NETWORKING PROTOCOLS + DOCKER

EPISODE D2.1 — TCP and UDP

Speaker A: Yesterday we ended with IP.

Speaker B: So today we ask what happens after the packet reaches the right host.

Speaker A: Exactly. The new question is:

Speaker B: Which service is listening, and what transport contract does it expect?

Speaker A: The visual is:

Speaker B: Endpoint -> Port -> Transport -> Delivery -> Evidence.

Speaker A: Good. Keep that model. It separates several things that people often call the network.

Speaker B: IP identifies the destination host.

Speaker A: Roughly, yes. Then a transport port identifies the service endpoint on that host.

Speaker B: And TCP or UDP tells us how the application data is carried.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Make a prediction.

Speaker A: A server has the correct IP address and my packets reach the host, but no application is listening on TCP port 8080. What should I expect?

Speaker B: The service connection should fail at the transport endpoint. If the host is reachable and the port actively refuses the connection, I may see connection refused.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: That is different from a timeout.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So our first lesson is to stop saying simply, 'the network is broken.'

Speaker A: Yes. Say which layer produced the evidence.

Speaker B: Stage one: endpoint.

Speaker A: An application wants to communicate with another service.

Speaker B: It needs a destination address and a service endpoint.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: Stage two: port.

Speaker A: A transport port identifies the service endpoint on a host.

Speaker B: So two services can use different ports on the same IP address.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: What about sockets?

Speaker A: A socket is the operating-system communication endpoint used by an application. Think of it as the application-side representation of the transport communication.

Speaker B: So a port is part of the endpoint identity, while a socket is the operating-system communication object.

Speaker A: Good enough for this lesson.

Speaker B: Stage three: transport.

Speaker A: Here TCP and UDP differ.

Speaker B: TCP is connection-oriented.

Speaker A: Yes. It establishes connection state and gives applications ordered, reliable byte-stream delivery.

Speaker B: Through acknowledgements and retransmission.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: And sequence numbers.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Flow control and congestion control too.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So TCP is doing a lot of work to give the application a stronger abstraction than raw packets.

Speaker A: That's the point.

Speaker B: What about UDP?

Speaker A: UDP sends datagrams without TCP's connection and retransmission machinery.

Speaker B: So UDP is not 'bad TCP'.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: It gives the application a simpler contract.

Speaker A: Exactly. That can be useful when the application wants low overhead, can tolerate loss, or implements its own recovery logic.

Speaker B: So saying 'UDP is faster' is too vague.

Speaker A: Yes. Speed depends on the whole application and network behavior. The useful statement is that UDP provides a different transport contract.

Speaker B: Human example?

Speaker A: Imagine a live sports commentary.

Speaker B: Latest score now may matter more than replaying every missed sentence.

Speaker A: Right. A different application may need every byte in order.

Speaker B: Such as a file transfer.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Stage four: delivery behavior.

Speaker A: TCP tries to turn an unreliable packet network into an ordered byte stream for the application.

Speaker B: Lost data can be retransmitted.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Data can be reordered before the application sees it.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Flow control prevents the sender from overwhelming the receiver.

Speaker A: And congestion control reacts to network conditions so the sender does not simply push as fast as possible.

Speaker B: So the transport layer is actively managing risk.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Another prediction.

Speaker A: A TCP server is listening. The client gets a timeout. Is that the same evidence as connection refused?

Speaker B: No. Refused means something responded with a refusal at the connection point. A timeout means the expected response did not arrive within the allowed time.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: The underlying cause could be very different.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: A firewall could drop traffic. A route could be wrong. The service could be absent. The endpoint could be overloaded.

Speaker A: Those are different hypotheses.

Speaker B: So timeout does not prove one specific failure.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: Another failure: the connection establishes, but the application returns a 500.

Speaker A: What does that tell you?

Speaker B: Transport worked well enough for the application to receive the request. The failure is now at the application layer.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So 500 is very different from timeout.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: What about connection reset?

Speaker A: It means the established connection was actively terminated rather than simply receiving no response. The next question is which component reset it and why.

Speaker B: So the words are evidence, not final answers.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Let's build the full path.

Speaker A: Client.

Speaker B: Destination IP.

Speaker A: Port.

Speaker B: TCP or UDP.

Speaker A: Application protocol.

Speaker B: Application response.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: And each boundary produces a different kind of evidence.

Speaker A: That's the model.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: First inspect listening TCP ports on your machine.

Speaker B: Predict what the result means.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then make a localhost TCP connection to a known listening service.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then try a known-closed TCP port.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Compare the evidence.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then make a small UDP experiment.

Speaker A: Observe the key difference: a UDP send does not require the same connection-establishment behavior as TCP.

Speaker B: So the experiment teaches the contract difference.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Controlled failure?

Speaker A: Stop a disposable local service.

Speaker B: Try the same TCP connection again.

Speaker A: Predict the new evidence.

Speaker B: Observe it.

Speaker A: Restart the service.

Speaker B: Verify the connection works again.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: What should I record?

Speaker A: Endpoint. Port. Transport. Expected behavior. Actual failure signal. Recovery evidence.

Speaker B: So the lab is a mini incident.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Recall.

Speaker A: What does a port identify?

Speaker B: A transport service endpoint on a host.

Speaker A: What is a socket?

Speaker B: An operating-system communication endpoint used by an application.

Speaker A: What does TCP add?

Speaker B: Connection state, ordered reliable byte-stream delivery, acknowledgements, retransmission, flow control and congestion control.

Speaker A: What is UDP's key difference?

Speaker B: It provides datagrams without TCP's connection and reliability machinery, leaving more trade-offs to the application.

Speaker A: Why is timeout different from refusal?

Speaker B: A refusal is active evidence at the connection point; a timeout means the expected response did not arrive in time.

Speaker A: What does an HTTP 500 tell you after TCP succeeds?

Speaker B: The application layer returned an error. It is not the same as a transport timeout.

Speaker A: Final challenge.

Speaker B: A server IP is reachable. The expected TCP port times out.

Speaker A: Your model?

Speaker B: Endpoint -> Port -> Transport -> Delivery -> Evidence. I first distinguish route and host reachability from the transport failure, then inspect whether traffic is being dropped, whether the service is listening, and finally the application response if transport succeeds.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So TCP and UDP are not just names to memorize.

Speaker A: They are transport contracts with different behavior and different evidence.

Speaker B: And the lab makes that visible.

Speaker A: Exactly. Make the local service fail, predict the signal, recover it, and explain what changed.

EPISODE D2.2 — DNS

Speaker A: DNS maps names to information. It is a distributed naming system, not a magical Internet phonebook stored in one server.

Speaker B: People usually remember DNS only when a website stops working.

Speaker A: Which is unfortunate because DNS is everywhere.

Speaker A: Think of a hotel reservation system. You remember the hotel name; a backend system resolves that name into a concrete destination. DNS does something analogous for network names.

Speaker A: Learn recursive resolvers, authoritative servers, A and AAAA records, CNAME, TTL, caching, and negative answers.

Speaker B: Joke: DNS is proof that humans invented names because remembering eight groups of numbers was emotionally unacceptable.

Speaker A: Technically accurate enough to be dangerous.

Speaker A: Real-life example: changing a DNS record is not always instantly visible everywhere because cached information can live according to TTL.

Speaker A: Lab: use dig to inspect a real domain. Ask the resolver different questions and observe record types and TTL.
Speaker B: DNS seems simple until someone says “it must be DNS” and then starts restarting things.

Speaker A: Yes. DNS is a distributed naming system, and diagnosis gets easier when you know which part of that system answered your question.

Speaker B: Resolver first.

Speaker A: Usually. A recursive resolver may answer from cache or go ask authoritative servers on your behalf. The authoritative server holds the authoritative data for a zone.

Speaker B: So when I use dig, I'm not necessarily talking directly to the owner of the domain.

Speaker A: Correct. That's why you should know what server you are asking.

Speaker B: TTL is the part everyone vaguely remembers.

Speaker A: Think of a shop posting a new opening time. You don't want every customer calling the shop every thirty seconds, so information is cached for a while. TTL is part of the mechanism that tells caches how long the answer may be retained.

Speaker B: And negative answers can be cached too.

Speaker A: Right. Which is why “I fixed the DNS record” does not necessarily mean every client immediately sees the new answer.

Speaker B: Prediction pause. I change an A record, and one machine still gets the old address. Do I immediately conclude the DNS server is broken?

Speaker A: No. First ask which resolver that machine uses, whether the answer is cached, what TTL applies, and whether the client has its own caching behavior.

Speaker B: So you trace the path of the answer.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Real-life example?

Speaker A: Hotel reservations. You update the booking information at the hotel, but a travel site may still show the old room count for a while because it cached the previous answer. The exact mechanics differ, but the operational idea is the same: information can have freshness rules.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Ask for A, AAAA, and CNAME records with dig. Look at TTLs. Ask a specific resolver. Then change nothing. Just observe. The goal is to stop treating DNS as one invisible black box called “the Internet.”

Speaker B: That's probably the real skill.

Speaker A: Yes. Name the component. Ask it a precise question. Read the answer.


EPISODE D2.3 — HTTP

Speaker A: HTTP gives applications a common language over a transport. Requests, responses, headers, status codes, bodies, cookies, and connection behavior all matter.

Speaker B: So HTTP is not the network itself.

Speaker A: Correct. It rides over transport and the underlying network path.

Speaker A: Think of a courier package. The network gets the package from city to city. HTTP is the information inside the package describing what the application wants.

Speaker A: A 404 is not the same thing as a TCP timeout. A 500 means something different from a DNS failure. Layer separation is your diagnostic superpower.

Speaker A: Real-life example: ordering food online. DNS finds the restaurant system, TCP/TLS establishes communication, HTTP carries 'I want this order,' and the application responds with a result.

Speaker B: That is much easier to remember than forty slides of status codes.

Speaker A: We still need the status codes. We just gave them a job.

Speaker A: Lab: use curl to inspect headers, status codes, redirects, and a JSON API.
Speaker B: HTTP is where application engineers feel at home.

Speaker A: Which is exactly why I want you not to skip the layers underneath it.

Speaker B: Right. A browser request starts with naming, transport, security, then application behavior.

Speaker A: Usually. And each layer can fail independently.

Speaker B: So a 404 is not a network outage.

Speaker A: Correct. A 404 is an HTTP response from an HTTP server. That tells you quite a lot already.

Speaker B: And a 500 means the application or server encountered an internal problem.

Speaker A: Usually, yes, though the precise source depends on the system generating it. The key is that you have received an HTTP response. You are no longer looking at a pure connectivity failure.

Speaker B: Real-life example?

Speaker A: Order a meal online. DNS gets you to the restaurant's system. TCP gives you a reliable transport. TLS protects the connection. HTTP says, “I'd like this particular thing.” The application decides whether it exists, whether you're authorized, and whether it can complete the request.

Speaker B: And a 401 versus 403?

Speaker A: Different questions. One is about authentication context; the other is about permission to perform the action. Again, don't flatten the whole system into “the request failed.”

Speaker B: Prediction. If curl gets a 301, did the network fail?

Speaker A: No. You received an HTTP response telling you that the resource is elsewhere. curl can follow the redirect when instructed. The important lesson is that application protocols can intentionally tell you to make another request.

Speaker B: So status codes are evidence.

Speaker A: Exactly. They're the server talking back.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Use curl to inspect response headers, follow a redirect, and ask for a JSON endpoint. Read the headers before reading the body. Look for status, content type, location, caching, and any request identifiers.

Speaker B: It turns a black-box request into a conversation.

Speaker A: That's what HTTP is. A structured conversation carried over lower layers.


EPISODE D2.4 — TLS and HTTPS

Speaker A: HTTPS combines HTTP with TLS. TLS provides cryptographic protection and server identity mechanisms.

Speaker B: Real-life analogy?

Speaker A: Imagine speaking to a person through a secure room with an identity badge at the door. The badge helps establish who you are talking to; the room protects the conversation from casual listeners.

Speaker A: Certificates, certificate authorities, public/private keys, and the handshake make that possible.

Speaker B: So encryption is not the same as authentication.

Speaker A: Exactly. You can encrypt data without correctly authenticating the endpoint. Secure systems care about both.

Speaker A: Real-life example: entering banking credentials into a fake website with a convincing logo. TLS is not a magic guarantee that the business itself is trustworthy; certificate validation is part of authenticating the endpoint.

Speaker A: Lab: inspect the certificate chain of a real HTTPS site with browser tools and command-line tools.
Speaker B: TLS is one of those topics where people say “encryption” and stop.

Speaker A: And then they wonder why certificate warnings are so serious.

Speaker B: So TLS has more than secrecy.

Speaker A: Yes. It also establishes cryptographic parameters and provides mechanisms for authenticating the endpoint through certificates and the trust system around them.

Speaker B: Public key, private key, certificate authority.

Speaker A: Right. You don't need to become a cryptographer this week, but you do need the mental model. Your client needs confidence that it established the secure session with the intended endpoint, not just with some random machine willing to encrypt traffic.

Speaker B: Real-life version?

Speaker A: Imagine walking into a secure building. The locked door protects the conversation inside, but the badge at reception helps you establish who you're actually talking to.

Speaker B: And a lock without identity could protect my conversation with the wrong person.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Prediction pause. The certificate is valid cryptographically, but the hostname doesn't match. What should you think?

Speaker A: Don't think “close enough.” Think authentication failure. The certificate may be valid for a different identity.

Speaker B: And if the certificate is expired?

Speaker A: Again, evidence that the trust conditions were not met.

Speaker B: Banking example?

Speaker A: A fake site can look visually identical to the real one. TLS doesn't promise that every business behind a valid certificate is morally good. It helps authenticate the endpoint according to the certificate and trust model.

Speaker B: That's a distinction people miss.

Speaker A: Constantly.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Inspect a real HTTPS certificate chain in your browser, then use command-line tooling to inspect the same endpoint. Don't just admire the certificate. Identify the subject, issuer, dates, and hostname relationship.

Speaker B: So the exercise is learning what the browser is quietly doing for me.

Speaker A: Exactly. Once you understand it, HTTPS stops being a green padlock and becomes a sequence of verifiable steps.


EPISODE D2.5 — Why Containers Exist

Speaker A: A container is an isolated process environment, not a virtual machine.

Speaker B: So the container is the apartment, and the process is the person living inside it?

Speaker A: A reasonable analogy. The important technical difference is that containers share the host kernel rather than emulating a full guest kernel.

Speaker A: Images package filesystem layers and metadata. Containers are running instances created from those images.

Speaker A: Real-life example: a food-delivery kitchen may have standardized stations and ingredients. You do not redesign the entire building every time a new order arrives. You create a repeatable working environment.

Speaker B: And that is why 'works on my machine' starts becoming less persuasive.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker A: Lab: build an image, run it, inspect its filesystem and process, and compare the image definition with the live container.
Speaker B: Containers. Let's kill one misconception immediately: a container is not a tiny virtual machine.

Speaker A: Good. A container is an isolated process environment using the host kernel. That distinction explains a lot of behavior.

Speaker B: So the image is the packaged filesystem and metadata, and the container is the running instance.

Speaker A: That's a useful working model.

Speaker B: What problem are we buying with that isolation?

Speaker A: Repeatability, packaging, dependency boundaries, and a clearer execution environment. Not perfect isolation, not a new physical computer.

Speaker B: Real-life example?

Speaker A: A commercial kitchen with standardized stations. You can replace one station without rebuilding the entire restaurant. The environment is repeatable.

Speaker B: And “works on my machine” becomes harder to defend because the environment is described.

Speaker A: Exactly. Though containers don't magically eliminate configuration bugs. They just make more of the environment explicit and reproducible.

Speaker B: Prediction. My container exits immediately after I start it. Does that mean Docker is broken?

Speaker A: No. First ask what the main process did. Containers are tied to their main process lifecycle. If that process exits, the container normally stops.

Speaker B: So I should inspect the command and logs before blaming the runtime.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Build a small image. Run it. Inspect the image layers and the container process. Then deliberately make the command exit. Watch what happens. You're learning the relationship among image, container, and process, not just learning docker run syntax.

Speaker B: Which is why the motion diagram says Image → Container → Process.

Speaker A: Exactly. The picture is a memory aid for a causal relationship.


EPISODE D2.6 — Docker Networking and Storage

Speaker A: Now connect the networking theory to containers.

Speaker B: So all those IPs and ports were secretly preparing us for this.

Speaker A: Precisely. A container can have a network namespace and an address inside a Docker network. Port publishing connects host-side access to a container service.

Speaker A: Think of an apartment complex. Internal apartment numbers work inside the complex. Visitors enter through the building entrance. Docker network addressing and published ports are different paths.

Speaker A: Volumes solve another problem: data that must outlive a container process.

Speaker A: Real-life example: throwing away a restaurant kitchen staff should not destroy the restaurant's inventory database.

Speaker B: That's a slightly dramatic way to explain a volume.

Speaker A: You remembered it, though.

Speaker A: Lab: run a frontend, API, and database. Break the database hostname, then break the published port, and identify why the symptoms differ.
Speaker B: Now the networking day finally connects to Docker.

Speaker A: This is where your earlier work pays off. Container networking is easier once ports, addresses, routing, and name resolution are already familiar.

Speaker B: Inside a Docker network, containers can discover one another.

Speaker A: Through the mechanisms Docker provides, yes. And port publishing is a separate concern: exposing a container service through the host.

Speaker B: So container port eighty and host port eighty are not automatically the same thing.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: Apartment complex again?

Speaker A: Apartment numbers work inside the building. Visitors still need an entrance. Internal service discovery and published access are different paths.

Speaker B: Storage has a similar boundary.

Speaker A: Exactly. The writable layer of a container is associated with that container's lifecycle. Persistent application data usually belongs in a volume or another external storage mechanism.

Speaker B: Meaning if I delete and recreate the database container, I shouldn't expect the database files inside the disposable container filesystem to survive.

Speaker A: Correct. That's one of the first painful lessons people learn.

Speaker B: Prediction. Database container is healthy. Application says “cannot resolve database.”

Speaker A: What does that point you toward?

Speaker B: Name resolution or network configuration before database credentials.

Speaker A: Good. Now change the symptom: the hostname resolves, but the TCP connection is refused.

Speaker B: Different hypothesis. Something is reachable, but the service isn't accepting connections on that port.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Run a tiny frontend, API, and database stack. Break the database hostname. Then restore it and break the published port. Compare the evidence. Finally remove the database container while keeping the volume and verify what survives.

Speaker B: So the lab makes the abstractions collide with reality.

Speaker A: That's the idea. Otherwise Docker remains a vocabulary lesson.


EPISODE D2.7 — Break Docker

Speaker A: The fastest way to learn containers is to break them deliberately.

Speaker B: So today the syllabus is failure.

Speaker A: Correct. Wrong port. Wrong hostname. Wrong environment variable. Permission failure. Unhealthy dependency. Resource exhaustion.

Speaker A: Real-life example: debugging a restaurant that is 'closed' could mean no chef, locked door, missing electricity, broken refrigerator, or a failed payment terminal. The symptom is the same; the cause is not.

Speaker B: So we need evidence that separates hypotheses.

Speaker A: Exactly. Check process, logs, network, configuration, health, and resource state in a deliberate order.

Speaker A: Lab: introduce one failure at a time and predict the visible symptom before observing it.

Speaker B: And if my prediction is wrong?

Speaker A: Excellent. That is where learning begins.
Speaker B: This is my favorite kind of lesson: break it on purpose.

Speaker A: Good. The trick is to break one variable at a time.

Speaker B: Wrong port first.

Speaker A: Then wrong hostname. Then environment variable. Then permissions. Then a dependency that is alive but not ready.

Speaker B: The important part is predicting the symptom before I touch anything.

Speaker A: Yes. Otherwise you're just performing random mutations and calling them experiments.

Speaker B: Real-life example?

Speaker A: A restaurant is “closed.” But is the staff missing? Is the front door locked? Is the kitchen without power? Is the payment terminal down? Is the building open but online ordering broken?

Speaker B: Same customer symptom. Completely different failure paths.

Speaker A: That's the entire job.

Speaker B: Prediction. Wrong hostname.

Speaker A: What do you expect?

Speaker B: DNS or service-discovery failure from the client. The database process itself might be perfectly healthy.

Speaker A: Good. Wrong port?

Speaker B: Depends. If nothing is listening there, connection refused. If traffic can't reach the destination, maybe timeout.

Speaker A: Better. Now add a bad credential.

Speaker B: The TCP connection can succeed, TLS might succeed if present, and the application then returns an authentication error.

Speaker A: Exactly. That's why layered diagnosis matters.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Create a tiny stack and keep notes. For every break, write four things: your hypothesis, the predicted observation, the actual observation, and the next test. At the end, erase the notes and repeat from memory.

Speaker B: That last part is mean.

Speaker A: It's called retrieval practice.

Speaker B: Same thing, nicer branding.

Speaker A: Fine. Call it supervised cruelty.

Speaker B: And that's apparently our educational philosophy.

Speaker A: Only on Tuesdays.
