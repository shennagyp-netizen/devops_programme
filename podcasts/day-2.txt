EPISODE D2.1 — TCP and UDP

Speaker A: Yesterday we ended with IP.

Speaker B: So today we ask what happens after the packet reaches the right host.

Speaker A: Exactly. The new question is:

Speaker B: Which service is listening, and what transport contract does it expect?

Speaker A: The visual is:

Speaker B: Endpoint -> Port -> Transport -> Delivery -> Evidence.

Speaker A: Good. Keep that model. It separates several things that people often call the network.

Speaker B: IP identifies the destination host.

Speaker A: Roughly, yes. Then a transport port identifies the service endpoint on that host.

Speaker B: And TCP or UDP tells us how the application data is carried.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Make a prediction.

Speaker A: A server has the correct IP address and my packets reach the host, but no application is listening on TCP port 8080. What should I expect?

Speaker B: The service connection should fail at the transport endpoint. If the host is reachable and the port actively refuses the connection, I may see connection refused.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: That is different from a timeout.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So our first lesson is to stop saying simply, 'the network is broken.'

Speaker A: Yes. Say which layer produced the evidence.

Speaker B: Stage one: endpoint.

Speaker A: An application wants to communicate with another service.

Speaker B: It needs a destination address and a service endpoint.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: Stage two: port.

Speaker A: A transport port identifies the service endpoint on a host.

Speaker B: So two services can use different ports on the same IP address.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: What about sockets?

Speaker A: A socket is the operating-system communication endpoint used by an application. Think of it as the application-side representation of the transport communication.

Speaker B: So a port is part of the endpoint identity, while a socket is the operating-system communication object.

Speaker A: Good enough for this lesson.

Speaker B: Stage three: transport.

Speaker A: Here TCP and UDP differ.

Speaker B: TCP is connection-oriented.

Speaker A: Yes. It establishes connection state and gives applications ordered, reliable byte-stream delivery.

Speaker B: Through acknowledgements and retransmission.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: And sequence numbers.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Flow control and congestion control too.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So TCP is doing a lot of work to give the application a stronger abstraction than raw packets.

Speaker A: That's the point.

Speaker B: What about UDP?

Speaker A: UDP sends datagrams without TCP's connection and retransmission machinery.

Speaker B: So UDP is not 'bad TCP'.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: It gives the application a simpler contract.

Speaker A: Exactly. That can be useful when the application wants low overhead, can tolerate loss, or implements its own recovery logic.

Speaker B: So saying 'UDP is faster' is too vague.

Speaker A: Yes. Speed depends on the whole application and network behavior. The useful statement is that UDP provides a different transport contract.

Speaker B: Human example?

Speaker A: Imagine a live sports commentary.

Speaker B: Latest score now may matter more than replaying every missed sentence.

Speaker A: Right. A different application may need every byte in order.

Speaker B: Such as a file transfer.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Stage four: delivery behavior.

Speaker A: TCP tries to turn an unreliable packet network into an ordered byte stream for the application.

Speaker B: Lost data can be retransmitted.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Data can be reordered before the application sees it.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Flow control prevents the sender from overwhelming the receiver.

Speaker A: And congestion control reacts to network conditions so the sender does not simply push as fast as possible.

Speaker B: So the transport layer is actively managing risk.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Another prediction.

Speaker A: A TCP server is listening. The client gets a timeout. Is that the same evidence as connection refused?

Speaker B: No. Refused means something responded with a refusal at the connection point. A timeout means the expected response did not arrive within the allowed time.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: The underlying cause could be very different.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: A firewall could drop traffic. A route could be wrong. The service could be absent. The endpoint could be overloaded.

Speaker A: Those are different hypotheses.

Speaker B: So timeout does not prove one specific failure.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: Another failure: the connection establishes, but the application returns a 500.

Speaker A: What does that tell you?

Speaker B: Transport worked well enough for the application to receive the request. The failure is now at the application layer.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So 500 is very different from timeout.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: What about connection reset?

Speaker A: It means the established connection was actively terminated rather than simply receiving no response. The next question is which component reset it and why.

Speaker B: So the words are evidence, not final answers.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Let's build the full path.

Speaker A: Client.

Speaker B: Destination IP.

Speaker A: Port.

Speaker B: TCP or UDP.

Speaker A: Application protocol.

Speaker B: Application response.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: And each boundary produces a different kind of evidence.

Speaker A: That's the model.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: First inspect listening TCP ports on your machine.

Speaker B: Predict what the result means.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then make a localhost TCP connection to a known listening service.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then try a known-closed TCP port.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Compare the evidence.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then make a small UDP experiment.

Speaker A: Observe the key difference: a UDP send does not require the same connection-establishment behavior as TCP.

Speaker B: So the experiment teaches the contract difference.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Controlled failure?

Speaker A: Stop a disposable local service.

Speaker B: Try the same TCP connection again.

Speaker A: Predict the new evidence.

Speaker B: Observe it.

Speaker A: Restart the service.

Speaker B: Verify the connection works again.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: What should I record?

Speaker A: Endpoint. Port. Transport. Expected behavior. Actual failure signal. Recovery evidence.

Speaker B: So the lab is a mini incident.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Recall.

Speaker A: What does a port identify?

Speaker B: A transport service endpoint on a host.

Speaker A: What is a socket?

Speaker B: An operating-system communication endpoint used by an application.

Speaker A: What does TCP add?

Speaker B: Connection state, ordered reliable byte-stream delivery, acknowledgements, retransmission, flow control and congestion control.

Speaker A: What is UDP's key difference?

Speaker B: It provides datagrams without TCP's connection and reliability machinery, leaving more trade-offs to the application.

Speaker A: Why is timeout different from refusal?

Speaker B: A refusal is active evidence at the connection point; a timeout means the expected response did not arrive in time.

Speaker A: What does an HTTP 500 tell you after TCP succeeds?

Speaker B: The application layer returned an error. It is not the same as a transport timeout.

Speaker A: Final challenge.

Speaker B: A server IP is reachable. The expected TCP port times out.

Speaker A: Your model?

Speaker B: Endpoint -> Port -> Transport -> Delivery -> Evidence. I first distinguish route and host reachability from the transport failure, then inspect whether traffic is being dropped, whether the service is listening, and finally the application response if transport succeeds.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So TCP and UDP are not just names to memorize.

Speaker A: They are transport contracts with different behavior and different evidence.

Speaker B: And the lab makes that visible.

Speaker A: Exactly. Make the local service fail, predict the signal, recover it, and explain what changed.

EPISODE D2.2 — DNS

Speaker A: Yesterday we separated IP, transport and application behavior.

Speaker B: Today we deal with the thing that turns a human-friendly name into network information.

Speaker A: DNS.

Speaker B: And the picture is:

Speaker A: Name -> Resolver -> Cache -> Authority -> Freshness.

Speaker B: So DNS is not one server.

Speaker A: Correct. It is a distributed naming system.

Speaker B: Make a prediction.

Speaker A: I change a DNS A record. One machine still receives the old address. Does that automatically prove the authoritative DNS server is broken?

Speaker B: No. The machine may be using a resolver that still has the older answer cached.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So the first question is: which resolver actually answered me?

Speaker A: Good. That is the beginning of the evidence path.

Speaker B: Stage one: name.

Speaker A: The application starts with a name such as a hostname.

Speaker B: The application does not need to know the final IP before asking DNS.

Speaker A: Right. It asks the naming system for the information associated with the name and record type it needs.

Speaker B: Stage two: resolver.

Speaker A: Most clients normally query a recursive resolver.

Speaker B: The resolver may already know the answer.

Speaker A: Or it may need to do more work and query authoritative DNS servers.

Speaker B: So when I use dig, the server answering my query may be a resolver rather than the authoritative server for the domain.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: That distinction matters when diagnosing a changed record.

Speaker A: Very much.

Speaker B: Stage three: cache.

Speaker A: A resolver can keep answers in cache for a period controlled by TTL and its caching policy.

Speaker B: So changing the authoritative record does not mean every resolver instantly changes its answer.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: And the cache is useful because the resolver does not need to repeat the whole lookup every time.

Speaker A: Exactly. Caching reduces repeated work and improves response time.

Speaker B: Prediction.

Speaker A: Two clients use different recursive resolvers. The authoritative record changed five minutes ago. Can the clients temporarily receive different answers?

Speaker B: Yes, depending on cache state, TTL and resolver behavior.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So different DNS answers do not automatically mean one server is lying.

Speaker A: Correct. You have to compare which resolver answered and whether its cache is fresh enough.

Speaker B: Stage four: authority.

Speaker A: Authoritative servers hold the configured DNS data for a zone.

Speaker B: So if I want to know the authoritative answer, I need to inspect the authoritative side rather than only the local recursive cache.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Which gives us another diagnostic question.

Speaker A: Go on.

Speaker B: Does the authoritative server have the value I expect?

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: If yes, and my resolver gives me an older value, caching becomes a stronger explanation.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: What about record types?

Speaker A: Different record types answer different questions.

Speaker B: A maps a name to IPv4 address information.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: AAAA maps a name to IPv6 address information.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: CNAME points one name at another canonical name.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So asking for the wrong record type can produce an answer that looks confusing even though DNS is working correctly.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Another prediction.

Speaker A: A name has both A and AAAA records. One client prefers IPv6 and another uses IPv4. Can the two clients behave differently even when DNS is healthy?

Speaker B: Yes. They may receive or prefer different address families and then take different network paths.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So DNS answers are inputs to later networking decisions.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Stage five: freshness.

Speaker A: TTL helps determine how long a resolver may retain an answer before it should refresh it.

Speaker B: So TTL is part of the freshness contract.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: What about negative answers?

Speaker A: Negative answers such as a name not existing can also be cached according to DNS rules.

Speaker B: Which means I can fix a missing record and still see the old negative result for a while.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: That is a very good incident trap.

Speaker A: It is.

Speaker B: So DNS diagnosis is not just: run nslookup.

Speaker A: Right. Start with the question.

Speaker B: Which name? Which record type? Which resolver? What does it return? What does the authoritative server say? What freshness rules apply?

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Let us make an incident.

Speaker A: You changed `api.example.com` from one IPv4 address to another.

Speaker B: My laptop still gets the old address.

Speaker A: What is your first test?

Speaker B: Query the resolver my laptop is actually using and inspect the TTL on the answer.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then ask an authoritative server for the same record.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: If the authoritative answer is new but the recursive resolver answer is old, I have strong evidence of caching.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: If both are old, I investigate the authoritative data or whether the change reached the intended DNS zone.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: That is much better than waiting and hoping.

Speaker A: Evidence first.

Speaker B: Controlled experiment?

Speaker A: Use a disposable local DNS environment or a test domain you control.

Speaker B: Query the current record.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Change the test record.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Query through the resolver and record the answer plus TTL.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Query authoritative data where available.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Restore the original record.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then verify the final state after the relevant cache window or with a deliberately isolated test environment.

Speaker A: Exactly. The point is to observe freshness behavior safely, not to change a production domain and hope everyone notices.

Speaker B: Good.

Speaker A: What about `dig`?

Speaker B: It lets me ask DNS focused questions and see the server response, record type, answer and timing information.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: So I can make the query itself explicit.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: `dig example.com A`.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: `dig example.com AAAA`.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: `dig example.com CNAME`.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: And I can ask a specific resolver by specifying the server.

Speaker A: Exactly. That is useful when two clients appear to disagree.

Speaker B: Prediction pause.

Speaker A: The authoritative server shows the new A record. Resolver one still shows the old address. Resolver two shows the new one. What does that suggest?

Speaker B: Different cache state or refresh timing between the two resolvers.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Another one.

Speaker A: A query for a missing name returns a negative answer on one resolver even after the record was created. What do you investigate?

Speaker B: Negative caching, resolver identity and the freshness period before changing anything else.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Ask for A, AAAA and CNAME records with dig.

Speaker B: Record which resolver answered.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Record the TTL.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Ask a specific resolver and compare the answer.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Where practical, compare with authoritative data.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then create one safe change in a disposable DNS environment.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Predict the cache behavior before you query again.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Then restore the original value and prove the expected final answer.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: What should success look like?

Speaker A: You can explain why a resolver answered the way it did without saying simply, 'DNS is slow.'

Speaker B: I should be able to name the resolver, record type, authoritative answer, cache state and TTL.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Recall.

Speaker A: What is DNS?

Speaker B: A distributed naming system that maps names to information through resolvers, authoritative servers and cached answers.

Speaker A: What does a recursive resolver do?

Speaker B: It answers client queries, often from cache, or performs the work needed to get the answer from authoritative DNS servers.

Speaker A: What does an authoritative server provide?

Speaker B: The authoritative DNS data for its zone.

Speaker A: What is TTL?

Speaker B: A freshness control that helps determine how long a resolver may retain an answer before refreshing it.

Speaker A: Why can two clients see different answers?

Speaker B: They may use different resolvers or have different cache state, and the answers may still be within their freshness rules.

Speaker A: Why can a fixed NXDOMAIN or other negative result persist?

Speaker B: Negative answers can also be cached.

Speaker A: Final challenge.

Speaker B: I changed a DNS record. My laptop still gets the old address.

Speaker A: Your model?

Speaker B: Name -> Resolver -> Cache -> Authority -> Freshness. I identify the resolver, inspect the record type and TTL, compare the cached answer with authoritative data, and then decide whether cache freshness explains the difference.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So DNS problems are often answer-path problems.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Run the lab, compare resolvers, and explain why the answer is what the client sees.

EPISODE D2.3 — HTTP

Speaker A: We now know how names resolve and how transport reaches a service.

Speaker B: Now we need the application conversation itself.

Speaker A: HTTP.

Speaker B: The picture is:

Speaker A: Request -> Headers -> Route -> Response -> Evidence.

Speaker B: So HTTP is not the network.

Speaker A: Correct. HTTP is an application protocol carried over lower layers such as transport, and often TLS.

Speaker B: Make a prediction.

Speaker A: A server returns HTTP 404. Did the TCP connection fail?

Speaker B: No. The client received an HTTP response. The application layer is telling us the requested resource was not found in that application's current routing/data context.

Speaker A: Good. The exact meaning depends on the application, but the key point is that you have application-layer evidence.

Speaker B: Stage one: request.

Speaker A: An HTTP request has a method, a target path and headers, and may have a body.

Speaker B: GET, POST, PUT, DELETE and others.

Speaker A: Yes. The method expresses the kind of operation the client is asking for.

Speaker B: And the path identifies what resource or route the client wants.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Stage two: headers.

Speaker A: Headers carry metadata around the request and response.

Speaker B: Content type.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Authorization context.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Cache directives.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Redirect information.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So headers are part of the protocol behavior, not just decoration.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: Another prediction.

Speaker A: An API route is correct, but the request sends the wrong Content-Type. Can the application still return an error even though TCP and TLS are working?

Speaker B: Yes. The lower layers can work while the application rejects the request shape.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Stage three: route.

Speaker A: The server or application selects a handler for the request's host, method and path.

Speaker B: This is application routing, not IP routing.

Speaker A: Very important. Same word, different layer.

Speaker B: So if `/orders/123` returns 404, I can investigate the application route before blaming the network.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Stage four: response.

Speaker A: The server returns a status code, response headers and, when appropriate, a body.

Speaker B: Status code becomes evidence.

Speaker A: Yes. 2xx means success according to the application protocol contract. 3xx describes redirection. 4xx usually describes a problem with the request or its authorization context. 5xx usually indicates server-side failure.

Speaker B: Usually.

Speaker A: Yes. Different systems can use specific codes in different ways, so learn the standard meaning and then inspect the actual application's contract.

Speaker B: That is more accurate than memorizing '404 means the server is broken.'

Speaker A: Much more accurate.

Speaker B: 404 and 500 are both HTTP responses.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: A TCP timeout is not.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: A DNS failure is not.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: A TLS certificate error is not an HTTP response either.

Speaker A: Exactly. That layer separation is one of the most useful habits in debugging.

Speaker B: Prediction.

Speaker A: curl receives 301. Did the server fail?

Speaker B: Not necessarily. A 301 is an HTTP response telling the client that the resource has moved to another location.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: If curl follows redirects, it can make another HTTP request.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: What about 401 and 403?

Speaker A: They are different application-layer signals. A 401 usually means the request lacks valid authentication credentials for the resource. A 403 says the server understood the request but is refusing access.

Speaker B: So the actual authorization model still matters.

Speaker A: Yes. Read the application's behavior and headers rather than turning codes into slogans.

Speaker B: What about JSON?

Speaker A: JSON is one possible representation carried in the HTTP message body. Content-Type helps tell the client how to interpret it.

Speaker B: So `application/json` is protocol metadata.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Now make an incident.

Speaker A: Users say the order page is broken.

Speaker B: First question?

Speaker A: What did the user actually receive?

Speaker B: Browser status? API status? Response body? Timing?

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Suppose curl gets 404 immediately.

Speaker B: I move down to route, transport and lower-layer evidence.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker A: That is why HTTP status is valuable evidence.

Speaker B: Controlled failure?

Speaker A: Use a local HTTP service you control.

Speaker B: Start with a known-good endpoint.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Change the route so the endpoint returns 404.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Predict the response before calling it.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then restore the route and prove the user path works again.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Another experiment: keep the route correct but send the wrong method.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Predict a different application response, such as 405 when the server uses that standard signal.

Speaker A: Exactly. The exact code is application-specific, but the experiment teaches method-aware routing.

Speaker B: And headers?

Speaker A: Send an intentionally wrong Content-Type to a local API endpoint and observe how the application reacts.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Use curl.

EPISODE D2.4 — TLS and HTTPS

Speaker A: We have HTTP. Now we need to protect that application conversation.

Speaker B: TLS.

Speaker A: Right. And HTTPS is HTTP carried inside a TLS-protected connection.

Speaker B: The picture is:

Speaker A: Client -> Certificate -> Handshake -> Secure Session -> Evidence.

Speaker B: So the certificate is not the entire TLS story.

Speaker A: Correct. It is one part of the endpoint identity check.

Speaker B: Make a prediction.

Speaker A: The connection is encrypted, but the certificate hostname does not match the hostname the client requested. Should the client trust the endpoint as intended?

Speaker B: No. Encryption alone does not prove I reached the intended server.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: That is the first big idea.

Speaker A: Encryption and authentication are related, but they are not the same claim.

Speaker B: Stage one: client.

Speaker A: The client wants a protected connection to a specific hostname.

Speaker B: So the hostname matters before the handshake is complete.

Speaker A: Yes. The client needs to know who it intended to reach.

Speaker B: Stage two: certificate.

Speaker A: The server presents a certificate chain.

Speaker B: The client checks whether that certificate identifies the requested hostname and whether the chain is trusted.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Dates matter too.

Speaker A: Yes. Validity dates are part of the certificate checks.

Speaker B: And the chain leads to a trusted root.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: What is the private key doing?

Speaker A: The server proves control of the private key associated with the certificate during the TLS exchange.

Speaker B: So a certificate file alone is not enough.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: Prediction.

Speaker A: A certificate is mathematically valid, but it is issued for `other.example.com` and the client requested `api.example.com`. What should happen?

Speaker B: The identity check should fail.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Stage three: handshake.

Speaker A: During the TLS handshake, client and server negotiate cryptographic parameters and establish the session keys used to protect application traffic.

Speaker B: So the handshake creates the protected channel.

Speaker A: Yes, while also carrying the peer-authentication process.

Speaker B: The exact handshake details get deep.

Speaker A: They do. At Beginner and early Intermediate level, the key model is enough: validate identity, negotiate the cryptographic session, then carry HTTP inside it.

Speaker B: Stage four: secure session.

Speaker A: Once the handshake succeeds, HTTP can travel through the protected TLS session.

Speaker B: So a 404 now means something different from a certificate failure.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: A 404 means TLS already got far enough for an HTTP response.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: A certificate hostname error means the client rejected the secure endpoint identity before normal HTTP behavior.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: That is the same layer-separation habit again.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Stage five: evidence.

Speaker A: The client can inspect certificate identity, issuer, validity dates and handshake outcome.

Speaker B: And then the HTTP response gives another layer of evidence.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: So I should not stop at the green padlock.

Speaker A: No. Understand what the client actually verified.

Speaker B: Real-life example?

Speaker A: Imagine entering a secure building.

Speaker B: Locked door first.

Speaker A: The lock protects the room.

Speaker B: And a badge helps establish who is allowed at the door.

Speaker A: Exactly. A locked room does not help if you entered the wrong building.

Speaker B: Nice.

Speaker A: Now make the failure cases.

Speaker B: Expired certificate.

Speaker A: The certificate's validity period is no longer acceptable.

Speaker B: Wrong hostname.

Speaker A: The certificate identity does not match the destination name the client requested.

Speaker B: Untrusted issuer.

Speaker A: The client cannot establish the expected trust chain.

Speaker B: Private-key problem.

Speaker A: The server may be unable to prove control of the key needed for the certificate.

Speaker B: Handshake failure.

Speaker A: The TLS negotiation cannot complete.

Speaker B: And HTTP 500 after TLS succeeds.
