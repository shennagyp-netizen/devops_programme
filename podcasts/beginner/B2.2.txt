EPISODE B2.2 — When "Everything Is Slow" Is Not Enough

Speaker A: We have a support message.

Speaker B: Please don't say it.

Speaker A: "Everything is slow."

Speaker B: There it is.

Speaker A: What does that tell us?

Speaker B: Someone is unhappy.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: And almost nothing else.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So we need a way to turn a vague complaint into evidence.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Logs, metrics, health checks.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Maybe traces later.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Let's keep the first model simple.

Speaker A: A metric gives us a number over time.

Speaker B: CPU, memory, request rate, latency.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: A log gives us an event or message.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: A health check gives us a focused answer to a focused question.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So they are different tools.

Speaker A: Different views of the system.

Speaker B: Give me a clinic example.

Speaker A: A lab manager says, "Patients are waiting too long."

Speaker B: That's the human version of "everything is slow."

Speaker A: Yes. Now ask what part is slow.

Speaker B: Registration?

Speaker A: Maybe.

Speaker B: Sample collection?

Speaker A: Maybe.

Speaker B: Analyzer?

Speaker A: Maybe.

Speaker B: Result review?

Speaker A: Maybe.

Speaker B: So the first metric we need could be total wait time by step.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Then we can see where the delay grows.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: Same thing for an application.

Speaker A: Yes. One endpoint can be slow while another is fine.

Speaker B: One database query can be slow while the rest are fine.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So averages can hide problems.

Speaker A: Very important.

Speaker B: Explain.

Speaker A: Suppose 99 requests take 50 milliseconds and one takes 20 seconds.

Speaker B: Average might still look okay.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: But one user had a terrible experience.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So latency distribution matters.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: p50, p95, p99.

Speaker A: Those become useful as systems get serious.

Speaker B: Good. We can say them without making the lesson hard.

Speaker A: That's the goal.

Speaker B: What about logs?

Speaker A: Logs tell us what the application says happened.

Speaker B: But logs can lie?

Speaker A: They can be incomplete, wrong, or too noisy.

Speaker B: So logs are evidence, not truth from heaven.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: I like that.

Speaker A: Do not use any signal like a magic answer.

Speaker B: Metric says CPU high. People restart.

Speaker A: Classic.

Speaker B: Log says timeout. People blame the network.

Speaker A: Classic.

Speaker B: Health check says green. People say everything is fine.

Speaker A: Also classic.

Speaker B: So we need to connect signals.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Let's build a simple incident.

Speaker A: B2 has an API. Users say order creation is slow.

Speaker B: What do we know?

Speaker A: Request latency for /orders went from 300 ms to 4 seconds.

Speaker B: Good. Now we have a real symptom.

Speaker A: Database latency also went from 20 ms to 3 seconds.

Speaker B: Interesting.

Speaker A: CPU on the API stayed normal.

Speaker B: So CPU is probably not the first place to focus.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: The database path looks suspicious.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Logs might show slow queries.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Maybe a new release changed a query.

Speaker A: Maybe.

Speaker B: Or traffic increased.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: Or the database is waiting on storage.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: So we still have several hypotheses.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: This is a proper incident.

Speaker A: Exactly. We have evidence, but not a final diagnosis.

Speaker B: What next?

Speaker A: Pick the next observation that separates the top hypotheses.

Speaker B: Compare query latency before and after the release.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Check request rate.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Check database CPU and storage behavior.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Look at slow-query logs.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So we are not collecting everything. We are collecting evidence that changes the decision.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: That is a big difference.

Speaker A: It is.

Speaker B: Pause.

Speaker A: Go ahead.

Speaker B: Make a prediction. API latency jumps from 300 ms to 4 seconds. API CPU stays low. Database latency jumps at the same time. Where do you look first?

Speaker A: Think before you answer.

Speaker B: Database path.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Not because the database is proven guilty.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: Because the evidence makes it a useful next place to inspect.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: So diagnosis is not "I know the cause."

Speaker A: It is often "I know the next useful observation."

Speaker B: That's a strong rule.

Speaker A: It is one of the best rules in debugging.

Speaker B: What if the database metric is wrong?

Speaker A: Then you compare another signal.

Speaker B: Maybe trace timings.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Or logs.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: So observability is really about being able to answer questions about system behavior.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Not collecting dashboards because dashboards look professional.

Speaker A: Thank you.

Speaker B: Some dashboards have twenty graphs and still don't answer one question.

Speaker A: You have seen this before.

Speaker B: We all have.

Speaker A: A good dashboard begins with a question.

Speaker B: "Are requests failing?"

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: "Are they slow?"

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: "Which endpoint?"

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: "Which dependency?"

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: "Which users?"

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: Then the signals follow.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Let's talk about health checks.

Speaker A: Okay.

Speaker B: Why not use one health endpoint for everything?

Speaker A: Because different checks answer different questions.

Speaker B: Liveness.

Speaker A: Is the process alive?

Speaker B: Readiness.

Speaker A: Can the service receive traffic?

Speaker B: Maybe dependency checks.

Speaker A: Sometimes. But be careful.

Speaker B: Why?

Speaker A: If every optional dependency turns the health check red, you can make the service look dead when it is actually able to do useful work.

Speaker B: So health checks need a clear purpose.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: Another real-life example?

Speaker A: A restaurant.

Speaker B: Okay.

Speaker A: The kitchen has power.

Speaker B: Liveness.

Speaker A: The kitchen is staffed and can take orders.

Speaker B: Readiness.

Speaker A: One freezer has a problem.

Speaker B: That affects part of the menu.

Speaker A: Right. It does not always mean the whole restaurant should close.

Speaker B: Nice.

Speaker A: In software, the same idea matters. A dependency problem may affect one feature without making the whole service useless.

Speaker B: So we need health signals that match actual service decisions.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: What about traces?

Speaker A: A trace follows one request across components.

Speaker B: So instead of seeing just total latency, we can see where the time went.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: API 50 ms. Database 3 seconds.

Speaker A: Or DNS 10 ms, network 20 ms, service 40 ms. Whatever the system gives you.

Speaker B: Then traces help connect the layers.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker B: This will matter more in the Intermediate course.

Speaker A: Yes. Beginner should understand the idea, not build a full tracing platform yet.

Speaker B: Good.

Speaker A: Now let's create a misleading signal.

Speaker B: I like those.

Speaker A: API CPU goes from 20% to 80%.

Speaker B: Everyone panics.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: But request latency did not change.

Speaker A: Good.

Speaker B: So maybe 80% is normal at this traffic level.

Speaker A: Correct.

Speaker B: The metric is not automatically bad.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: A number needs context.

Speaker A: Yes.

Speaker B: Another classic.

Speaker A: The error rate is zero.

Speaker B: Great.

Speaker A: But the service returns 200 with an empty order list.

Speaker B: So application correctness is broken while transport looks healthy.

Speaker A: Exactly.

Speaker B: That is nasty.

Speaker A: Production likes that kind.

Speaker B: Very funny.

Speaker A: I was not joking.

Speaker B: Even worse.

Speaker A: Right.

Speaker A: Yes. A technically healthy request can still be a bad user experience.

Speaker A: It does.

what question does it answer?

Speaker A: Good.
