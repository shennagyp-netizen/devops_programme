DAY 1 — LINUX + NETWORKING FOUNDATIONS



EPISODE D1.1 — Linux Operating Model



Speaker A: Before we touch DevOps tools, let's talk about the machine underneath the drama. Your application is ultimately a collection of processes asking an operating system for CPU time, memory, files, and network access.

Speaker B: So the cloud is just a very expensive machine room with better marketing?

Speaker A: That's a dangerous oversimplification... but a useful starting point.

Speaker A: Think about a restaurant. The customer sees the waiter carrying food. Behind that waiter are the kitchen, refrigerators, staff, inventory, electricity, and a manager deciding what gets attention first. A Linux process is one active piece of work. The kernel arbitrates access to shared resources.

Speaker B: And when the restaurant becomes slow, yelling at the waiter is probably not root-cause analysis.

Speaker A: Exactly. You inspect the kitchen.

Speaker A: Linux gives processes identifiers, memory, file descriptors, scheduling, signals, filesystem access, and network sockets. The shell is itself a process; it asks the kernel to launch or control other processes.

Speaker B: File descriptors always sound like something invented by people who hate newcomers.

Speaker A: They are just numbered handles to communication channels. Standard input, standard output, and standard error are the famous ones. Pipes connect channels so processes can compose.

Speaker A: When you run ps, top, lsof, kill, or inspect logs, you are asking the system to reveal state.

Speaker B: Real-life version?

Speaker A: If a clinic says, 'the lab is slow,' the useful questions are: Which machine? Which process? CPU? Memory? Disk? Network? One user or everyone? That is the same diagnostic mindset.

Speaker A: Mac lab: inspect processes and listening sockets. Pick one harmless process, predict what happens if it stops, then verify.



Recall: What is a process? What is the kernel responsible for? What is a file descriptor? Why is the shell not the kernel? What evidence do you collect before blaming the machine?



EPISODE D1.2 — Terminal as an Engineering Tool



Speaker A: The terminal is not a collection of spells. It is a compositional language for interrogating and manipulating a system.

Speaker B: I know the commands but sometimes type them like a medieval wizard.

Speaker A: Most engineers have done that. The trick is understanding what each command contributes.

Speaker A: Imagine a detective with a clipboard. ps tells you who is alive. grep filters evidence. tail watches recent events. find searches. A pipe hands output from one investigator to another.

Speaker B: So ps | grep something is two detectives passing notes.

Speaker A: Precisely. Except the detectives are processes, and nobody gets paid overtime.

Speaker A: Review pwd, ls, cd, find, grep, cat, less, head, tail, awk, sed, pipes, redirection, environment variables, ssh, curl, and process inspection.

Speaker A: Real-life example: 'The service is down.' First ask the machine: Is the process alive? Is a port listening? What does the log say? Can the local endpoint answer?

Speaker B: That is triage.

Speaker A: Exactly. The command line becomes a fast triage instrument.

Speaker A: Lab: inspect a harmless process, redirect output, filter a list, then query a closed local port and study the evidence.



EPISODE D1.3 — Processes, Services, Permissions and Logs



Speaker A: A production service has a lifecycle. It starts, runs, consumes resources, emits evidence, and eventually stops or restarts.

Speaker B: Like an employee, except the employee can be restarted automatically.

Speaker A: Please do not give management ideas.

Speaker A: Think of a hotel. Guests are requests. Rooms are resources. Staff are processes. The front desk is a service endpoint. The supervisor keeps the system operating when one worker disappears.

Speaker A: In Linux, understand process state, signals, users, groups, permissions, environment variables, services and logs.

Speaker B: Why can a perfectly healthy application still fail?

Speaker A: Because it may not be allowed to read a file, bind a socket, access a directory, or obtain a credential.

Speaker A: Real-life example: a lab technician knows the correct file exists but cannot open a locked cabinet. The data exists; authority is missing.

Speaker A: Lab: inspect a service-like process, its environment, files and logs. In a temporary directory, make one permission change and observe the failure.



EPISODE D1.4 — What Is a Network?



Speaker A: Now we rebuild networking from the ground up.

Speaker B: I know IP addresses, but the layers around them have always felt like a group project where nobody introduced themselves.

Speaker A: Start at the link layer. Ethernet moves frames. MAC addresses identify interfaces on a local link. Switches move frames inside a LAN. ARP helps map local IP addresses to MAC addresses.

Speaker A: Real-life analogy: an apartment building. The apartment number matters after the building is known. Local delivery needs local addressing.

Speaker A: Then IP gives network-level addressing. Do not confuse MAC, IP, and a TCP port.

Speaker B: So if DNS is broken, the Ethernet cable can still be perfectly healthy.

Speaker A: Correct. Different layer, different failure.

Speaker A: Lab: inspect your Mac's interfaces, addresses, gateway and nearby network information.



EPISODE D1.5 — IP Addresses, Subnets and CIDR



Speaker A: IP addressing gets easier when you stop treating CIDR as a decorative slash.

Speaker B: The slash has always looked like a fraction that escaped into networking.

Speaker A: It is a compact way to state how many leading bits define the network portion.

Speaker A: Imagine a neighborhood. The street identifies a shared area; the house number identifies one home. A subnet is the neighborhood boundary.

Speaker A: We will reason through 192.168.1.0/24, 192.168.1.0/26, and 10.0.0.0/16, calculating boundaries and understanding why segmentation matters.

Speaker A: Real-life example: a large hospital does not need every device in one undifferentiated network. Departments and sensitive systems may be separated to control communication.

Speaker A: Lab: calculate CIDR ranges manually before using a calculator, then compare with the machine.



EPISODE D1.6 — Routing, Gateways and NAT



Speaker A: Routing answers: where should this packet go next?

Speaker B: Like Google Maps, except the packet is not allowed to ask for coffee.

Speaker A: And it does not appreciate scenic routes.

Speaker A: A route table contains decisions about destinations and next hops. The default gateway handles traffic that does not match a more specific route.

Speaker A: Think of a courier company. The local driver does not know every street in every city. They know which distribution center receives the package next.

Speaker A: NAT changes addressing at a boundary. Do not confuse NAT with routing or security.

Speaker A: Lab: inspect your Mac's routing information and trace a destination. In a safe environment, change one route and predict the effect.

Speaker B: So the practical rule is: identify the destination, the next hop, and what changes at the boundary.

Speaker A: Exactly.
