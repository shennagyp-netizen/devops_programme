EPISODE A3.2 — The Trade-off Review

Speaker A: We have built the system.

Speaker B: Great.

Speaker A: Now let's make the decision uncomfortable.

Speaker B: I knew there was a catch.

Speaker A: Every large system has trade-offs.

Speaker B: Latency.

Speaker A: Consistency.

Speaker B: Availability.

Speaker A: Cost.

Speaker B: Operational complexity.

Speaker A: Exactly. That is the key step.\n\nSpeaker B: The mistake is trying to maximize everything at once.

Speaker A: Right. The point is not the command; it is the evidence.\n\nSpeaker B: Because improving one target can make another worse.

Speaker A: So architecture needs explicit choices.

Speaker B: Human example?

Speaker A: A shop wants every customer to get a fresh stock count.

Speaker B: Easy.

Speaker A: Every branch asks one central system before confirming a sale.

Speaker B: Fresh data.

Speaker A: Yes. The important part is what happens next.\n\nSpeaker B: But every sale depends on the central system.

Speaker A: And the network.

Speaker B: So availability can fall when the central path fails.

Speaker A: Correct. And there is another part to watch.\n\nSpeaker B: Alternative?

Speaker A: Let each branch keep local stock.

Speaker B: Faster.

Speaker A: Usually.

Speaker B: More available locally.

Speaker A: Maybe. I would treat that as a hypothesis, not a conclusion.\n\nSpeaker B: But now counts can disagree.

Speaker A: Exactly. That changes the diagnosis.\n\nSpeaker B: There is no free lunch.

Speaker A: Distributed systems serve the bill first.

Speaker B: Nice.

Speaker A: Let's make the review concrete.

Speaker B: Start with latency.

Speaker A: What does the user need?

Speaker B: Fast response for a page.

Speaker A: Fine.

Speaker B: Maybe 100 milliseconds for one request.

Speaker A: The exact target depends on the product.

Speaker B: Right.

Speaker A: Now consistency.

Speaker B: Does every read need the newest value?

Speaker A: Maybe not.

Speaker B: A product description can tolerate some delay.

Speaker A: Correct. That is an important distinction.\n\nSpeaker B: A payment state cannot safely behave like that.

Speaker A: Exactly. That is why the failure is useful.\n\nSpeaker B: So we classify data by business consequence.

Speaker A: Right. That is the assumption we need to test.\n\nWe need explicit choices, not a promise to maximize everything.\n\nSpeaker B: Availability.

Speaker A: What happens when a dependency is unreachable?

Speaker B: Fail closed?

Speaker A: Sometimes.

Speaker B: Serve stale data?

Speaker A: Sometimes.

Speaker B: Queue the request?

Speaker A: Sometimes.

Speaker B: Reject only one feature?

Speaker A: Sometimes.

Speaker B: Again.

Speaker A: The product decides what is safe.

Speaker B: Cost.

Speaker A: More replicas.

Speaker B: More regions.

Speaker A: More replicas of data.

Speaker B: More observability.

Speaker A: More network traffic.

Speaker B: More people needed to operate it.

Speaker A: Exactly. Now we have a real boundary.\n\nSpeaker B: A design can be technically possible and still be a bad business choice.

Speaker A: Right. Now the failure becomes observable.\n\nSpeaker B: Not because it is ugly.

Speaker A: Because the cost or operational burden is not justified by the user benefit.

Speaker B: That's a useful distinction.

Speaker A: Now complexity.

Speaker B: Multi-region systems add failure cases.

Speaker A: Caches add invalidation rules.

Speaker B: Queues add delayed work.

Speaker A: Retries add duplicate-work risk.

Speaker B: Replicas add consistency rules.

Speaker A: Every mechanism creates new behavior.

Speaker B: So the design review should ask not only "what does this improve?"

Speaker A: But also "what new failure does it create?"

Speaker B: Prediction.

Speaker A: Go.

Speaker B: We move a write from one region to three active regions.

Speaker A: What improves?

Speaker B: Local write latency.

Speaker A: Maybe. I would treat that as a hypothesis, not a conclusion.\n\nSpeaker B: What gets harder?

Speaker A: Coordination and conflict handling.

Speaker B: Good.

Speaker A: Another.

Speaker B: We add a cache in front of the database.

Speaker A: Improvement?

Speaker B: Lower read load and often lower latency.

Speaker A: New risk?

Speaker B: Stale data and cache invalidation.

Speaker A: Exactly. That is the key step.\n\nSpeaker B: Another.

Speaker A: Add aggressive retries.

Speaker B: Temporary failures may be hidden.

Speaker A: New risk?

Speaker B: Retry storms and longer request times.

Speaker A: Good. Now ask what the user sees when that path is unavailable.\n\nSpeaker B: So every architecture choice gets two columns.

Speaker A: Benefit.

Speaker B: Cost.

Speaker A: More columns if needed.

Speaker B: Failure modes.

Speaker A: Operational work.

Speaker B: User impact.

Speaker A: Correct. But we still need evidence.\n\nThe business consequence tells us how much staleness we can accept.\n\nSpeaker B: What about measuring the trade-off?

Speaker A: Each decision needs an observable outcome.

Speaker B: If we add a cache, measure hit rate and backing load.

Speaker A: Yes. Keep following the request path.\n\nSpeaker B: If we add replicas, measure read latency, lag and failover behavior.

Speaker A: Yes. That can change the load pattern.\n\nSpeaker B: If we add another region, measure cross-region traffic, user latency, recovery behavior and operating cost.

Speaker A: Exactly. That changes the diagnosis.\n\nSpeaker B: So architecture is not finished when the diagram looks good.

Speaker A: It is tested against behavior.

Speaker B: This is where SRE and architecture meet.

Speaker A: Exactly. That is why the failure is useful.\n\nThe architecture cannot decide that without the product rule.\n\nSpeaker B: Mac lab?

Speaker A: Take your A3 design.

Speaker B: Draw one table with five columns.

Speaker A: Latency.

Speaker B: Consistency.

Speaker A: Availability.

Speaker B: Cost.

Speaker A: Complexity.

Speaker B: For each major design choice, write the expected benefit.

Speaker A: Then write the new risk.

Speaker B: Then write one metric.

Speaker A: Then write one failure test.

Speaker B: Example?

Speaker A: "Add cache."

Speaker B: Benefit: lower read latency.

Speaker A: Risk: stale values.

Speaker B: Metric: cache hit rate and read latency.

Speaker A: Failure test: make the backing store slow or unavailable and observe behavior.

Speaker B: Good.

Speaker A: Then review the whole table.

Speaker B: Find one choice where two benefits are hiding one large cost.

Speaker A: That's the challenge.

Speaker B: Recall.

Speaker A: One. Why are trade-offs unavoidable?

Speaker B: System resources and failure behavior are limited, so improving one property can reduce another.

Speaker A: Two. Why classify data by business consequence?

Speaker B: Different data can tolerate different levels of staleness or unavailability.

Speaker A: Three. Why does complexity matter?

Speaker B: More mechanisms create more behavior, failure modes and operating work.

Speaker A: Four. What should every major architecture choice include?

Speaker B: The intended benefit, new risks, operational cost, user impact and measurements.

Speaker A: Five. When is a design decision really tested?

Speaker B: When its predicted behavior is checked against real measurements and controlled failure.

Speaker B: Final challenge?

Speaker A: Defend one A3 choice.

Speaker B: But do not say, "It is better."

Speaker A: Say what it improves.

Speaker B: Say what it makes worse.

Speaker A: Say how you will measure both.

Speaker B: And say what happens when the design assumption fails.

Speaker A: That is the level of reasoning we want.

Speaker B: Not perfect architecture.

Speaker A: Explicit architecture.

Speaker B: With evidence.

Speaker A: Exactly. Now we have a real boundary.\n\n
